# Chapter 10: The Steadiest Weeks

The four weeks that followed were the steadiest weeks of Drew's life.
He sold fish. He sold a great many fish. Mira paid him in small heavy stamped copper from the Mireval mint, with Aren's profile on one face and a stylized perch on the other. Drew kept the coin in a small leather pouch on his hip until the pouch began to drag, at which point Branwen, at the smithy, made him a slim wooden box for his counter. The system happily read it as an upgrade and converted his loose copper into a flat efficient *Coin: 47g* slot in his inventory.
He levelled. The Angler tree opened up, and he took Cast Accuracy II, then Lure Craft I, and discovered, on the back porch of the bait and tackle shop, that he had a quiet good eye for the construction of small spinners, which Mira taught him without teaching him by handing him scraps and watching what he did with them. He took Patience II, the passive, on a morning when Mira informed him that his stamina regen was going to need it because she had a list of co-op fishing techniques she wanted to try with him and most of them required him to sit very still for a long time. He hit Lv. 4 on the back of a carp he caught with Banjo's help, and hit Lv. 5 on a long, quiet, lake-trout afternoon a week later, and the level-up brought, when he opened the menu, a new Tier II skill list.
He took Fisherman's Mark.
FISHERMAN'S MARK (Lv. 1) Designate a single named fish on the lake or river. The marked fish becomes visible to your Fisherman's Sight at any range and through any obstacle. Marks persist until the fish is caught or until the next sunrise. One mark active at a time.
He had asked Mira, at some point in week three, whether his level cap was the same as hers. She told him she didn't know, that no protagonist she had ever heard of had hit the native-born cap of Lv. 9, and that the elders' correspondence suggested protagonists had different rules altogether: bigger ceilings, different unlocks at higher tiers, multiple skill picks per level at the early tiers, and abilities that did not exist in the native-born tree at all. He had noticed the multiple-picks one already on his own panel, where the level-up rolls had been generous and the Angler tree had filled out faster than Mira's level math should have predicted. He pushed the rest onto the later pile. It was the kind of thing he would find out about when he found out about it.
He had no immediate use for it. He took it because it was the most unusual option on the list and because there was a pleasing pragmatism to the idea that he could, if he ever needed to, make a particular fish unhide from him. He kept it tucked in the back of his head for later.
He picked flowers on his way back from the lake most evenings. The meadow at the south side of Mira's deck never thinned, and Branwen had pointed out a second meadow on the north path that was even better. He kept a steady stock of bouquets in his inventory because he had learned, by week two, that running out of them at the wrong moment was not a thing he wanted to do twice. The slot read, at any given time, somewhere between fifteen and thirty.
He paid Branwen.
He paid her in three increments. The first was the seventy gold, on a Tuesday, on the workbench of her shop, while she was planing a board. She accepted the coin without counting it, dropped it into a leather pouch on a hook above her bench, and said, "Now stay for a while and tell me about your week, Halloran." He stayed and told her about his week, and came back the next Tuesday and the Tuesday after that. By the end of the third Tuesday her affection was at 78 and *Tier: Companion*, her chipped-tooth grin came easier, and her shoulder bumped his on the workbench casually, like a friend's would.
He took a breath.
"Branwen," he said, "are we — is this... I mean."
She finished the long even pass she was making with the plane, set the plane down at right angles to the bench, and turned to look at him with the chip-toothed grin starting in the corner of her mouth and her ears tipped slightly forward.
"Listen, Halloran." She crossed her arms over her apron. "When the boathouse is done, you will sleep in it on the first night. I am not, on the first night, going to come over. Mira is going to, possibly. Lyselle, possibly. You and I will, on a different night, after, do our own thing in our own way. I am not racing. You are not racing me. The boathouse is the boathouse. Please ask me to come fishing with you sometime in the second week. That's what I want. Not a competition with the half-elf."
He swallowed. "Branwen, I — yeah. Okay. Yeah. Fishing in the second week."
"Bring beer," she said.
"Where do I get beer?"
"You go to my cousin's. Three doors past Iri's." She picked the plane back up. "Tell her I sent you. She'll overcharge you. Pay it."
"Branwen has a cousin?" Drew said.
"Branwen has nine cousins, Halloran. This is a village."
"How have I not met them?"
"Because I have been protecting you, dear." She gave him a sidelong grin over the plane. "Take a count of how many women you have already worked your way through and tell me whether you can handle nine more today."
He had laughed. She had handed him a small piece of board and asked his opinion on the grain. The number on her panel had not, that day, moved. It had not needed to. She was at 78 and it was a fine number, and he was grateful for a woman who had decided unilaterally that her own pace was not his to negotiate.
He met Mira's grandmother.
He met her in week three, on a Sunday afternoon that he had, in retrospect, been promised would happen sooner or later. She was sitting on a stool in the lane outside her own shop, which sold woven things, with a piece of yarn in her lap and a tortoiseshell cat on her shoulder, and she had stopped him with one finger when he tried to walk past her.
"Drew Halloran," she said.
"Yes ma'am," Drew said, stopping.
"I am Iren Holloway." She did not put down her yarn. "And I have been, in the order of my responsibilities, a bell-warden, a yarn-mistress, the maker of pickled trout that my granddaughter has copied twelve times and not gotten right, and a woman who is going to be straight with you about her Mira. Sit down."
He had sat down on the stoop.
"How old are you, son?" Iren asked.
"Thirty-one."
"My granddaughter, who has been twenty-two for two hundred and thirty-something years and who will, the gods willing, be twenty-two for several centuries more, is in love with you." She kept knitting as she spoke. "I am, on inspection, comfortable with this. You appear to be a man who knows how to clean a fish, who has not yet broken anything significant in this village, and who carries his dog dignifiedly enough to suggest the dog has trained you well. You will, in my analysis, do."
"Thank you, ma'am," Drew said.
"I am not, however, going to die for you, be sentimental for you, or give you my pickling brine recipe for a hundred and fifty years, minimum." She paused her needles long enough to look at him. "Are we clear."
"Crystal, ma'am."
"You have my granddaughter's heart on a plate, Halloran." She held his eye. "If you treat it like a plate, I will know, and I will, with these hands, ruin your boathouse before it floats. If you treat it like the whole girl, which is what it is, I will, when I am pleased with you, and not before, give you a single ladle of my brine to put on a single fish, and you will know whether it is worth waiting for. Yes?"
"Yes, ma'am," Drew said.
"Hand me that ball of yarn."
He had handed her the ball of yarn. She had taken it without looking at him, set the cat down on his lap, said, "Mind her, she bites, but only ankles," and resumed her knitting. Drew had sat on Iren Holloway's stoop for half an hour with a tortoiseshell cat purring on his thigh and Iren knitting beside him without saying another word. Iren had been the head of the Holloway line for forty years now, since whatever quiet kind of dying it was that took Mira's mother. Her panel had bloomed in his peripheral vision the moment she said his name, and was ticking steadily upward in the quiet.
She was, by the time he got up to leave, at 24 and Acquaintance.
He was, by the time he got up to leave, in love with her too, harmlessly so, the size and shape of a feeling he had not had since his actual grandmother had died. He had, walking back down the lane, made a small private vow that he was going to earn the brine.
He met other women too. Hazel arrived at the shop one morning with a beard ribbon she had made him, sturdy braided green thread, which he wore for a day and which she came back later to inspect and then ran away from, embarrassed by herself, before he could thank her properly. Vesha invited him to a fox-kin family supper, which was loud and featured a great many small foxes and three larger ones he was almost sure were Vesha's actual mother and aunts in less-talkative form. The supper ended with him having permission to fish in a specific cove of the lake that Vesha said was hers and which he was honored to be told was now his. Tessa and Little Tess turned out to be a pair of sisters who ran a weaving stall together on Saturdays and who were, between them, one of the funniest pairs of people Drew had ever met. Ophelia the selkie, who he had only glimpsed at his first welcome and who he had been waiting to meet again, slid up next to him at the chandler's bath night with an offer to teach him to swim properly. He accepted, and on a Wednesday at the second bell it started the only co-op skill chain in his menu that involved holding his breath underwater.
He went back to Iri's three more times, slowly, with chocolates, with fish, and once with a small wooden carving of a fish he had made under Mira's eye in the back room. The carving was the worst Mira had ever seen, and Iri received it with a dry, slow, pleased smile that suggested she had been waiting a while for somebody to bring her a thing he had personally made. The number ticked, not fast. By the end of week four she was at 22 and *Tier: Acquaintance*, and she had told him exactly twice that she was pleased he was being patient, which he was beginning to understand, in her vocabulary, was high praise.
He thought once, that month, about his ex-wife.
He thought about her grudgingly. He had been carrying that small grudging thought for almost a year and hadn't, until this morning, been ready to set it down. She had not been the storm or the lightning or the kayak. She had been a person he had not, in the years they were married, paid the attention he was now, every day, paying to a half-elf shopkeeper, a 240-year veteran elder, a cat-kin carpenter, and the names of fish he had never seen before. He was, he thought, sorry about that, and not in any position to tell her so, and possibly, if Lyselle was right about the editing, remembering a version of her that the system had smoothed for him. He hoped she was, wherever she was, okay, and let the thought go. The lake was bright. Banjo was asleep. The country station, somewhere on a different planet, was still playing in a truck he was never going to drive again.
The boathouse went up.
Branwen built it the way Branwen did everything, which was without hurrying and without missing. She had Drew there for the framing, which she insisted he do himself under her supervision, because she said the boathouse needed his hands in it for the system to flag it as his. She had the village do the raising on a Sunday morning with about thirty people present, including two of the cousins he had not yet been allowed to meet, and the structure went up slowly and practiced, the way a correctly planned building goes up. The pilings were sound, the deck was wide, and the wraparound porch was bigger than he had any business asking for. The two boat slips underneath were, Branwen said, *for the future you*, which Drew chose to interpret generously.
By the morning of the move-in, the boathouse was done.
It was empty, because the furniture had not been delivered yet, because Drew had not yet bought the furniture. The whole place smelled like new wood. There was a hand-tooled door knocker in the shape of a smallmouth bass, with a small inscription on the back where only Drew would ever see it, which read, in Branwen's handwriting, *DON'T FALL IN. — B.* The wraparound porch let you walk around in a slow circle and see the lake from every angle. Upstairs sat a bedroom with a window that looked east, and downstairs a sitting room with a stone hearth Branwen had pulled out of the river bottom in three pieces and reassembled the way you assemble a stove, which had taken her four days she would not let him pay for.
It was, in summary, the best house Drew Halloran had ever owned.
He stood on the wraparound porch on the morning of the move-in with his dog at his feet and a cup of the eighty-five-percent coffee in his hand, and he watched the lake, and he thought about the dream.
The dream had not gone away.
He had, every other night for four weeks, woken up sweating. None of the subsequent dreams had stuck the way that one had, but they had been the same shape. He had told Lyselle, who had written letters, and the elders had written back. None of the others had dreamed a named child, and none had dreamed a figure that refused to look at the protagonist. Lyselle had thanked them, folded the letters up, put them in the locked drawer of her writing desk, and told Drew to enjoy his boathouse.
He intended to enjoy his boathouse.
He also intended, quietly and over the long stretch, to be a man who was looking for the door. Not today, not tomorrow, but eventually, in the patient manner of an Angler who knew that the fish came when the fish came, he was going to find what was past where letters could follow.
For now, though, there was furniture to buy and an empty house to fill.
Banjo had decided in the last week that this porch was where he lived now. He sighed without opening his eyes.
Drew drank his coffee. The bell at the Hall rang the first of the day. Down the lane, the village began to wake up, easy and practiced, forty-some women who had been waiting his whole life for him to need them now starting their morning.
