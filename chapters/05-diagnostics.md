# Chapter 5: Diagnostics

The shop floor was harder than he had anticipated.
Mira had piled three quilts and a bolster pillow into a sort of nest in the corner behind the counter, with an oil lamp on a low shelf and Banjo in the curl of Drew's knees, which was charming in concept and a small infrastructure problem in practice. Banjo, having decided that Drew's body was a load-bearing wall in his own personal architecture, had positioned himself such that any movement Drew made was a movement against forty-five pounds of unconscious chocolate lab. Drew's hip went numb sometime around the second hour after midnight. He shifted. Banjo grunted. Drew lay still.
He stared at the dark beams of the ceiling and listened.
The shop creaked, the way old buildings creak, the same way his apartment creaked, the same way every building he had ever slept in had creaked. The creek outside ran its quiet running. Upstairs, very faintly, he could hear Mira breathing. He did not understand how he knew it was Mira and not the wind or a board settling. He knew it. He chose not to think about it. He was developing, he realized, a working folder in his head titled *things I will choose not to think about*, and the working folder was getting full.
He went to sleep eventually, and dreamed, and could not remember the dream when he woke up.
What he could remember was waking, sitting up in the dark before morning, his shirt soaked through, his hair damp at the temples, his heart going at the steady fast rate of a body that had run without his permission. He remembered the half-second after waking when he had been absolutely convinced, wrongly, that he was back in his own bed, his apartment, his Wisconsin, and that the entire previous day had been a nightmare he was now safely out of. Then came the next half-second, when his eyes adjusted and he saw the rafter beams, the swaying shadow of a hanging lure, the chocolate lab pressed warm against his thigh, and the conviction had gone out of him like air leaving a tire.
*Still here.*
He lay back down and did not sleep again.
Mira came down the stairs at first light with her hair flattened on one side and an oversized linen shirt of her father's hanging off one shoulder. She stopped at the bottom step when she saw he was awake, one hand still on the banister.
"You're still here," she said.
"Yeah," Drew said, from inside his quilts.
"I thought you'd have gone fishing already."
"It's early."
"It's late, for an Angler." She gave him a small unimpressed look, the kind of look she clearly reserved for men still in floor-quilts at dawn while mocking her routine, and came the rest of the way down.
She padded across the floor in her bare feet and dropped into a crouch beside his nest, peering down at him. The portrait at the corner of his vision, which had been off for the night, chimed quietly back online and yawned theatrically before settling into its usual cheerful watchfulness.
"Bad sleep?" she asked, tilting her head.
"Weird sleep."
"Dream?"
"I think so." He scrubbed a hand through his hair. "Can't remember it."
"That happens here." She said it the way she said most things, practically, like it was a known feature of weather. "The first few weeks, especially. Mom used to say the system pushes some of the orientation for new arrivals into dreams, because the panel can only do so much."
"That's reassuring."
"I'm not promising it's *that*," she said, pushing herself back up to standing. "I'm telling you it's a thing people say."
Drew dragged a hand down his face. "Mira, I would like coffee."
"I have a thing that's like coffee."
"How like coffee?"
"Eighty percent like coffee," she said, already turning toward the stove.
"I'll take it."
She put a kettle on. She did this, he was beginning to suspect, the way other people put on shoes. While the water heated she leaned a hip against the counter and watched him try to coax himself out of the quilts. She came back a few minutes later with two cups and pressed one into his hand. The thing in the cup was approximately eighty percent like coffee. The remaining twenty percent was, as far as he could tell, a flavor that did not exist on his planet, and which he was going to have to develop a relationship with on its own terms.
"Drink that," Mira said, settling cross-legged on the floor across from his nest. "Then go fishing."
"Right now?" Drew said into his cup.
"Take your starter rod out. Go down by the creek, the dock's yours for as long as you're staying with us. Or go to the river. Or go up the lake path. *Go* somewhere." She started ticking villagers off on her fingers. "Catch fish, level up. Meet some of the others. Go talk to Hazel, she'll lose her mind, she's been sitting in her garden trying to look casual for a full day waiting for you to find her. Go talk to the carpenter, you'll need her sooner than later. Go talk to Lyselle if you want, she likes the early ones. Don't come back until afternoon, or do, but go out first. You can't level on my dock all day."
"You want me out of the house," Drew said.
"I want you in the village."
"You sure?"
She held his eye for a beat too long over the rim of her cup, then set the cup down on the floor.
"Drew. I'm going to be very transparent with you for a minute."
"Okay."
"I would like you to keep staying with me. Until you build your own place. That's not me being polite. That's me wanting it. The shop has the room, we can set up something better than this floor situation tonight, and I would like, very much, if you stayed. But I would also like, also, very much, for you to have a real day in the village before you make any decisions, so that you're not staying with me because I'm the only person you've met. Do you understand the distinction I'm trying to make?"
"Yeah," Drew said.
"Yeah?"
"Yeah. I do." He gave her a small look over his cup. "That's — that's nice of you, Mira."
"It's also self-interested."
"How is it self-interested?" he asked, sitting up the rest of the way and folding his legs.
"Because if you spend a day out and you decide to come back here at the end of it, then I get to know you came back on purpose. And if you decide to stay with someone else, I get to know that too, and I get to be sad about it tonight in private and not later in a fight."
"You're telling me you're managing for the long view."
"I am extremely good at the long view, Drew. It's basically all I have."
He drank his coffee-thing, watching her over the rim of the cup. For a thing that wasn't coffee, it was doing coffee's job. Mira sat on the floor across from him with her cup balanced on her knee, watching him think.
"Okay," he said, eventually. "Okay. Build my own place. You said something yesterday — I have to build a house. That's a thing."
"That's a thing."
"Walk me through it."
She gathered her knees up to her chest before answering, settling in.
"You buy lumber from the carpenter. Or you cut your own logs and pay her to mill them and frame them, which is cheaper. You'll need gold. You'll need an axe, which she'll give you. Or sell you. She's flexible. You'll need to pick a spot — there's open lots all around, the village has plenty of empty land, you just claim one and the system flags it as yours. Then you and the carpenter work it out. Cabin, cottage, whatever. Branwen's been drawing protagonist houses in her sketchbook since she was a teenager just in case, so she'll have ideas."
"Why do I have to do this?" He set his cup down on the floor. "Why can't I — Mira, why can't I just couch-surf? Stay with you a couple weeks, stay with somebody else a couple weeks, be a guest in the village. People do that on Earth. Cheap and easy."
"You can't get married without a house."
He stopped, halfway through scratching Banjo's ear.
"Ah," he said.
"Yeah." She watched him over her cup.
"That's a — that's a hard system rule?"
"It's a hard system rule," she confirmed.
Drew rubbed his jaw. "The system requires me to own property to marry."
"Yes."
"The system is, structurally, a 1950s mortgage broker."
"I don't know what that is," Mira said, fighting a small twitch at the corner of her mouth, "but I have a feeling that face is one a lot of protagonists make, in other villages, when their elders explain it to them."
"*Why?*"
She set down her cup and pinched the bridge of her nose for a half-second, looking briefly like someone asked to defend a thing she had not personally written.
"Because of the system, Drew, I don't know why, I keep telling you, I don't know who wrote it." Her hand dropped. "The other thing is that the village just — *feels* better, when the protagonist has his own place. That's what the older travelers say, anyway. They've been to villages where it's already happened. They say the village settles, the day after a protagonist's house gets finished. People relax. It's a confirmation. He's staying. I've never felt it myself, but I want to. Build a house. People will be glad. I'll be glad."
"Branwen the carpenter," Drew said.
"Branwen the carpenter. Branwen, who works wood, lives on the south end of the lane, two doors past the bathhouse, blue door with a crossed-axe sign. You'll like her. Everyone likes her. Be careful — she's also a flirt."
"How careful?"
"Don't punch above your weight, Drew." She unfolded her legs and stood up, brushing her palms on the front of the linen shirt. "Have a day."
He drank the rest of his coffee-thing. He gathered up his rod from where Mira had leaned it against the counter. Banjo had finally levered himself off Drew's leg and was standing in the middle of the shop floor, alert and expectant, having heard the word *outside*, and was already starting his small impatient circles.
At the door Drew paused.
"Mira," he said, turning back. "I'll be back this afternoon."
She had her thumbs hooked in the pockets of her overalls and was watching him from across the floor of the shop.
"I know," she said.
"You don't, but I will."
She crossed the floor, bumped his shoulder with hers, and held his eye for a half-second longer than was strictly necessary.
"I'm choosing to know."
"Okay," Drew said.
"Go."
He went.
He did not, in the end, go to the river or up the lake path or to the carpenter's. He walked a hundred steps along the lane and then his feet, which had a working relationship with the rest of him that had served them all well over thirty-one years, decided on his behalf. He went down the alley between the shop and the smithy and came out on the back deck. The morning fog still hung over the water, the dock was empty, the rod rack held rods that were not his, and the bait jar on the bench was full of glossy sleeping worms. Banjo went down the steps to the dock, turned in a circle three times, and lay down with his nose at the edge.
Drew sat on the dock with his rod across his knees and didn't, immediately, fish. He looked at the creek, then at the soft fog lifting off the water, and thought.
He started, like any salesman would have started, with the customer. The customers, in this case, were the people he had met. There were Mira and Lyselle, who had each given him real information yesterday, and there was the village at large, which had given him noise. Mira was twenty-two, two centuries and change, half-elf, an Angler who was four levels his senior, a bait shop owner, a maker of unreasonable cheese. She liked him, and her liking him ticked up on a number visible to him and on metrics that were not yet visible. She had explained the metrics with a frankness that suggested she had nothing to hide from him about how she felt, and which had also, simultaneously, included a small careful duck about why her starting affection had been a higher than expected eight points. She had told him she didn't know who built the system, that the protagonists who'd tried to find out either hadn't come back or had come back and stayed silent. She had cried when he caught a fish on her dock. He did not, on balance, think she was lying to him about anything substantive, but he also did not think she was telling him everything she knew.
Lyselle was something else.
She was, Drew thought, the actual point of contact for the part of this place that was deliberate. She had been running the village for two and a half centuries, by her own count, after being trained by a previous elder named Aren, who had passed from being elder too long. She knew what a smartphone was, or had read enough correspondence to know the concept. She had told him, calmly, that he was dead, that there was no way home, and that the system was physics, and she had told him those things in a register he recognized as one a person fell into when they had given the same speech often enough to pare it down to the load-bearing words. She wasn't hiding from him so much as choosing, out of long practice, what to share and what to keep, and the keeping wasn't personal. He could work with her, level her up in the new and slightly nauseating language he was now obliged to think in, and the higher he leveled her the more she would tell him.
The carpenter was a stranger and a tool. He needed her for the house. He would also see, when he met her, whether she was a person.
The other girls, he had not yet met properly, and could not assess.
The system itself was, Drew thought, the thing that interested him the most in the long term and which he had the least access to. It told him exactly enough about its mechanics to operate it, and nothing about its provenance. It awarded him a hidden flag for asking the right questions, muted itself occasionally when grief happened, and had, somehow, returned his dog to him, which was either a kindness from the people behind the system or an insidiously well-targeted carrot. He did not know yet.
What he did know was that the only way to learn more was to play, and he did not love that as a conclusion. He cast.
The morning passed. He caught chubchirps and a brassy thing Mira had not had a name for, and a small bottom-feeder he was almost certain was a bullhead, and twice the rod went still and he watched a glowing blue outline approach his bait under the surface and then change its mind and drift away. Banjo woke up halfway through and watched the fish parade, quietly and professionally interested. The light got better and the fog burned off. Somewhere down the lane the bell at the Hall rang the third bell, and a few minutes after that the smell of woodsmoke from the smithy started. The portrait at the corner of his vision, who had nothing to look at because Mira was not present, sat in profile, watching the water with him.
The system chimed.
LEVEL UP — ANGLER LV. 2 You have earned 1 stat point and 1 skill slot. Allocate at your convenience.
He thought *skills*.
Six new options unfolded. He read them. He picked Cast Accuracy I, which the panel described as *improves rod handling and casting precision at short to medium range.* It appeared to be the least magical of the options and the most directly useful, and he had spent enough of his life trying to get a chatterbait into a six-foot pocket between two snags to know what an accuracy bonus was worth. He allocated his stat point to Perception.
He stood up, and Banjo stood up with him.
"All right," Drew said, dusting his palms on his trousers. "Let's go meet the carpenter."
Branwen's door was, as advertised, blue.
The crossed-axe sign was wrought iron, hand-forged, and quite good. Drew paused on the front walk to admire it, the way he had paused, several thousand times in his life, in front of well-made things. Then he knocked.
"It's open!" a voice called from inside.
He went in.
The front room of the carpenter's was not what he had expected. He had expected sawdust, beams, and stacked timber. There was sawdust and there were beams and there was timber, but there was also a daybed under a window with a fat striped cat asleep on it, a small bookshelf full of paperbacks Drew was almost certain had not been printed in this world, given the worn yellow spines, a teapot on a hot plate, a jar of wildflowers, and two windows thrown open to let the morning in.
Branwen herself was at a workbench, planing a long board, with her back to him.
She was tall, taller than Mira, bigger across the shoulders, longer in the arms, and carrying a working strength that registered to Drew before her face did. Her short, shaggy gray hair, he could already tell, was the same gray as the cat asleep on the daybed. The shirt she wore had its sleeves cut off at the shoulder and had clearly belonged, originally, to a man, but it fit her now in a manner that made it firmly her shirt. Her ears were pricked forward in concentration, twitching at the sound of the door. A long fluffy tail moved in slow patient arcs behind her, cat-concentrating-on-something arcs.
"Sorry," Drew said, hovering just inside the door. "I — Mira sent me. I'm Drew."
She straightened up slowly and turned around.
She held his eye for a long second, craftsman-style, top to bottom and back up again, and Drew had the distinct sensation of being assessed for grain, knots, and resale value. Her eyes were a very pale green, and she had a chip out of one of her front canines. She was also, he had to honestly admit, having been overwhelmed by beautiful women now for two days running, extremely pretty.
Then she grinned. The grin was wide and a little crooked and pulled at the chipped tooth.
"Drew," she said. Her voice was lower than he had expected, husky like she sang a lot or smoked sometimes or had simply been born with it. "Drew the Protagonist."
"Drew the just-Drew," he said.
"Drew the just-Drew. I like that." She set the plane down on the bench with a soft *tock*. "Mira told me you were coming. Mira told *everybody* you were coming, actually, she sent a list around last night by way of Hazel, I have it on my fridge. I'm Branwen. Hi."
She wiped her hand on her apron and stuck it out. He shook it. Her hand was bigger than his, which was, Drew thought, neither here nor there but did warrant noting. The portrait of Branwen that bloomed in his peripheral vision was the cleanest one he had seen yet, a small line drawing of her with her grin, her chipped tooth, and her ears pricked, clean black ink on cream, and a wink that was, somehow, more roguish than Mira's portrait had managed.
BRANWEN ASHFORD Race: Cat-kin | Age: 24 | Class: Carpenter
AFFECTION ▮▯▯▯▯▯▯▯▯▯ 4 / 100 Tier: Stranger
MOOD — [LOCKED] OPINION — [LOCKED] AROUSAL — [LOCKED] AVAILABLE REQUESTS — [LOCKED]
"Sit," Branwen said, jerking her chin at a stool by the bench. "Or stand. Whichever. You want tea? I've been told by reliable sources that you like tea now."
"I — sure. Sure, tea."
"Tea, dog?" she added, glancing down.
Banjo, having wandered in behind him, looked up at Branwen with his diplomatic face. The cat on the daybed opened one eye, assessed Banjo, closed it again, and was unbothered. A working professional acknowledging another working professional.
Branwen poured him tea, then herself, and kept moving while she did it, a little prowl-walk back and forth between hot plate and bench that was her body's idle state. She did not sit down, but gestured for him to.
"So," she said, leaning a hip against the bench. "House."
"House," Drew echoed.
"Mira said you'd want one."
"Yeah. Yeah, I — apparently I need one, to do anything around here."
"To get married, mostly. The rest of it you can do without. But yeah." She took a swallow of tea, kicked her hip against the bench, and grinned at him again. "You been thinking about it? What you want?"
"Floating boathouse," Drew said.
Her eyes lit up.
"Oh, *yes*." She set her cup down hard enough that it clinked against the bench. "Yes, please. Drew. *Drew, Drew.* I have been drawing imaginary protagonist houses in a sketchbook since I was nineteen years old, on the off chance one of you ever showed up, and the most exciting thing in any of those drawings has been a wraparound porch. *Floating boathouse.* You are spoiling me. Drew, are you going to let me put a wraparound porch on it?"
"I — yes?" Drew said.
"Are you going to let me put cantilevers off the back over the water?" She was bouncing on the balls of her feet now, which on a woman her size set the floorboards creaking.
"I would have to know what those words mean."
"Don't worry about it." She waved the question off. "Are you going to let me put two slips for boats underneath?"
"Two?"
"In case you make a friend."
He laughed. He couldn't help it. "Branwen, yes. Yes to all of it."
"Excellent." She set her tea down and dragged a sketchbook out from under her bench and was already drawing, fast, in pencil, while Drew watched. He saw the shape of a house come up out of the page in about thirty seconds, with a deck and a roofline and the suggestion of pilings in the water and a pair of doors set sliding into a wall. Drew had spent his career adjacent to people who were very good at things, and he recognized very-good-at-things.
She turned the sketchbook around. She had drawn it.
"That's — Branwen, that's a real building."
"It can be." She tapped the pilings with the tip of her pencil. "There's a stretch of bank between Mira's shop and the deep bend of the creek that's been waiting for one of these. Pilings will go through clean, the orientation's right for a wraparound, and the slips can run out into the bend without bothering the chubchirps. I'll claim it for you tomorrow. We have to talk gold. I have to work out a materials list. You have to get me logs." She glanced up at him over the sketchbook. "You any good with an axe?"
"Reasonable," Drew said.
"Reasonable is fine. You'll get better fast — Anglers do, the muscle memory crosses over from rod-work." She straightened up off her bench, crossed to a tool wall on the far side of the room, and pulled an axe down off a peg. "I'll give you my spare. It's a good axe. It'll like you."
Drew's eyebrows went up. "You're — you're giving it to me."
"It's a gift. I have like four. Take one." She held it out across the bench.
The number on her panel, where it hung beside her ear, ticked from 4 to 7. Drew blinked at it.
"Branwen, it just — your number went up," Drew said, watching the panel by her ear.
"I know." She was already wiping her hands on her apron, unbothered.
"You can't see it."
"No, but I'm doing the giving." She tapped her own chest with the pencil. "The giving makes me happier with you, which the system reads, which makes the number go up. You'll feel it later when somebody gives you a thing — you'll feel a little fonder of them. It's how the system rewards being generous. Apparently being generous is good for fondness, which, fair." She shrugged. "It's almost like the system has a point."
"Did you — sorry, you said the system has a point. Like it's a designed thing?"
She gave him a flat look over the top of her tea.
"Drew, sweetheart, I am a carpenter. I do not have time to brood."
"Got it," Drew said.
"Take the axe. Cut me trees. Bring back logs. We'll talk gold tonight." She was already rolling up the sketch.
"How many logs?" Drew asked.
"Twenty to start. Big ones, but not so big you can't move them. Fir or cedar if you can find them, pine if you have to. Don't bring me ash, ash floats wrong."
"And the gold," Drew said. "How much?"
"Seventy. To start. For the structure. More for the porch, more for the slips. I'm not gonna pretend it's cheap. I'll cut you a discount for the entertainment value of getting to build a floating house, but you'll still owe me real money."
"Where do I get gold?" he asked.
She laughed. It was a low laugh, real, easy.
"Sell fish, Drew. Sell fish to Mira, Mira pays you in gold and resells them to the village. Sell pelts to Hazel if you go up into the woods. Run errands. Take quests. The system will hand you bounties as you level up. You'll be fine. You're an Angler in a fishing town. You're going to be rich."
"Okay," Drew said.
"You also—" She caught herself, laughed at herself, and corrected. "Before you go cut trees, I want to give you a fish. I mean. *You're* going to give *me* a fish."
"Am I?" Drew said.
"You came in with one in your sack," she said, gesturing at his hip.
He had. He had come in with the smaller chubchirp in a small canvas sack at his hip, the one that had appeared on him yesterday and not yet been explained. He had forgotten about it. He pulled it out. It was, somehow, still as fresh as when he had caught it, and that was the kind of detail he had stopped questioning.
He held it out across the bench. "Here. For — for the axe."
"I won't say no to a chubchirp."
She took it, weighed it in her hand, and went to put it in a small icebox by the hot plate. The number on her panel ticked from 7 to 11.
"Tier: Acquaintance," Drew said, mostly to himself, watching the panel shimmer.
"Welcome to the club," Branwen called over her shoulder. "We have jackets."
"You don't have jackets."
"We don't have jackets. I lied." She straightened up from the icebox and dusted her hands. "I don't even have a club. Take your axe and go cut trees."
He took the axe.
It was a beautiful axe, double-bitted, the head a dark steel with a single small maker's mark stamped at the heel, and the haft a smooth pale wood that fit his hand like it had been waiting for him. He suspected, again, that the system was being kind to him, and that it was at least equally Branwen's eye for matching tools to the people who were going to hold them.
"Branwen, thank you," he said.
"Don't thank me yet. Come back with logs."
He came back, several hours later, with logs.
The woods north of the village were, he discovered, very pleasant to cut trees in.
The trees were cooperative. They were the wrong-different green he had noticed yesterday on the path, but their wood was real wood, and his axe bit into them properly, and they came down in directions he aimed them in. Banjo had elected himself foreman, and sat at a safe distance on a flat rock supervising unflappably, confident in the dog-specific way that this team had a clear leader and was not him.
Drew worked.
He had not done sustained physical labor since the divorce had eaten his gym habit, and he had forgotten how good it felt. The first tree was hard, the second harder, the third easier. He felled the third one cleanly, with a precise notch and a clean back-cut, and Banjo applauded, which was the only word for it. The dog woofed at him once, sharply, and wagged his tail in big slow arcs, and Drew, hot and breathing hard with sap on his hands, laughed out loud at him.
He stripped the branches and cut the trunks into rough logs. He had eight of them on the ground, lined up, sap-fragrant, when he realized he did not know how he was going to carry them back to the village.
"Banjo," he said, leaning on the axe and looking down the line of logs, "we have made an oversight."
The system chimed.
NEW MENU AVAILABLE — INVENTORY Items may be stored and retrieved from your inventory regardless of physical bulk. Fishing equipment, raw materials, and currency are tracked here.
Drew, sweating in a stand of strangely-green trees in another world, laughed for the second time that morning.
"Of course," he said to the trees. "Of course there is."
He thought *inventory*. The panel chimed. A clean grid unfolded itself in his peripheral vision, with slots, most of them empty. The second chubchirp from his sack, the starter rod, and the axe Branwen had given him each took their own slot, and six other little fish from the morning sat in further slots, each labeled by species. A small icon for *Coin: 0g* sat in the corner.
He thought *log* at the nearest log on the ground.
The log dissolved into motes of light and reappeared as an icon in a fresh slot, labeled *Fir Log (raw)*, with a small ×1 underneath it.
"Banjo," Drew said, "we are about to become extremely productive."
Banjo had no idea what was happening, but he supported the sentiment. He woofed.
Drew put eight logs into his inventory in the time it took to breathe and felt, in the body sense, that he had not just lifted eight logs, which he had not. He stood in the small clearing of stumps. He looked at the stumps. He thought, *trees regrow tomorrow*, and shook his head.
"Game logic," he said to no one. "Sure."
He sharpened his axe with a stone Branwen had given him, slung it over his shoulder, and headed back toward the village.
He was halfway back when he made the decision.
He had logs to deliver to a carpenter who was waiting for them, a long afternoon ahead of him, and a list of girls Mira had given him, half-jokingly, that he could in theory work through and meet. There were levels to gain, and quests, he assumed, he had not yet found.
He thought, instead, about the back dock.
He thought about Mira, specifically: her short black hair and the strip of ribbon she had pulled it back with this morning, her cut-off overalls, her face going pink yesterday when his butt-slap experiment had startled her. He thought about her affection number, which sat at 42, and which he had not yet finished raising. He pictured the *Tier: Friend* under it, and the readouts under that. Opinion sat amber at *Handsome Savior*, Mood soft green at *Pleased*, with both Arousal and Available Requests still blacked-out, all of them waiting for him to do enough of the right things to unlock them.
He had been told, in plain language, by every woman he had spoken to in the last twenty-four hours, that he was the village's protagonist. He had been told, also in plain language, that the system was a panel, the panel was the rules, and the rules were forgiving, because the make-up loop was, by Mira's own words, kind of the point. He had been handed, in summary, a video game and a gauge.
He was, he was willing to admit to himself, kind of stoked about it. He was thirty-one, recently divorced, and had spent eleven months waking up alone in an apartment with a country station he had not bothered to change, and he had been told he was now in a place where the women were NPCs running on a system that rewarded him for paying attention and forgave him for poking at the limits.
He noticed, while he walked, that the small voice in his head that would have flagged this on Earth was not flagging it now. The voice hadn't gone silent. It had read the rules, looked at the gauge, watched Mira coach him through the use of both, and decided to wait this out. He was, he was beginning to suspect, the kind of man who, given a forgiving sandbox, was going to find out what it forgave. He wasn't certain he liked that about himself, but he was certain he was going to do it anyway.
He was also aware, at the back of his head, that he was thinking about Mira right now in a different register than the one he had thought about her in over the cheese yesterday, or the one he had thought about her in when she said *yes* against his shoulder on the dock. The cheese-Mira and the yes-Mira had felt like a person. The Mira he was walking back to right now felt, for the duration of this walk, more like a panel. He noticed this, and didn't especially mind it. He intended to find out what the chart permitted, which was not a thing he could do by sitting on her dock catching chubchirps and being polite.
He started walking faster.
Mira was on the back deck when he came around the side of the building.
She had a small basket of laundry in her arms and was hanging shirts on a line strung between the deck rail and a cedar post, and she had her short black hair tied back with a strip of ribbon, and she was humming. She turned when she saw Drew, the wet shirt in her hands forgotten, and her face did the bright-immediate thing it had done yesterday on the path.
"You came back," she said.
"I told you I would."
"You did. I was choosing to know."
"How's the choosing-to-know?" Drew asked, climbing the steps onto the deck.
"Bumpy," she admitted, pinning the shirt up.
Banjo headed straight for his stove, an old friend at this point. Drew set the axe down by the rail. He sat on the bench next to where Mira had been hanging laundry and watched her finish a shirt.
He had decided, somewhere along the path back, that he wasn't going to ask her permission. That wasn't how this worked. The panel was the rule. The panel was the thing he was here to learn. Asking her would only get him an honest opinion from a person, and the opinion of the person was not the experiment.
The experiment was the system.
"Mira," he said, after a moment. "Come here a second."
She had a clothespin between her teeth and a shirt-shoulder pinched in each hand. She glanced sideways at him over the line, pinned the shoulder, set the basket down at her feet, and came around to stand in front of him, ducking under the laundry line, with her hands on her hips, sunny and game.
"Yes?" she said.
He stood up. He hugged her.
It was, to his own mild surprise, a perfectly normal hug. She fit neatly under his chin, and her arms came up around his shoulders and squeezed back without hesitation, the way a friend's hug squeezes. She smelled herbal-clean, the way she had yesterday, and her hair brushed his jaw. The number on her panel ticked from 42 to 44. The portrait at the corner of his vision was beaming with its hands clasped at its chest.
He did not let go.
He took his time with it. He slid the hand at the small of her back lower, deliberately, and palmed her where he had told himself, back there on the path, he was going to.
She made a small noise against his shoulder. *Hey.* She did not pull away, her arms stayed loose around his shoulders, but she squirmed a little, the kind of squirm a cat manages when it has been picked up wrong, and made the noise again, less muffled. *Drew.*
The number dropped from 44 to 37. The Mood readout, which had been a soft pleased-green, shifted to a flicker of pinker-amber and the word MOOD read *Annoyed.* The portrait at the corner of his vision crossed her arms in his peripheral vision while the actual Mira was still pressed against his ribs.
He held the hand there for another half-second, just to confirm the read, and let her go.
She stepped back, crossed her arms for real, exactly the way the portrait had, and raised one eyebrow. The annoyance on her face was, Drew could see it clearly now, exactly the annoyance her face had been wearing yesterday after the first slap, the same costume from the same drawer.
"*Drew.*"
"I know," he said, watching her panel.
"You are a *brat.*" She tipped her chin up at him, eyebrow still raised.
"I know."
"You owe me flowers." She said it the way a person says *that's two days you owe me on the rent*, annoyed, but with the annoyance already shelved somewhere it wasn't going to bother her again. "Go on."
"Working on it." He was already moving for the deck rail.
There was a meadow on the far side of the deck, sloping down toward the creek, full of small purple things and yellow things and white star-shaped things he was going to choose to assume were not poisonous. He picked a handful, came back, and held them out to her over the rail.
She took them. The portrait went pleased. The number ticked from 37 to 41.
She inspected the bouquet, plucked the purple ones out, and laid them across the top with the dignified focus of a small piece of organizing.
"Better," she said. "Three more like this."
He went back out and picked another handful, just the purple ones this time, because she had picked the purple ones out of the first bunch and put them in front. He gave them to her, and the number ticked from 41 to 46.
He went back out a third time and picked a handful with grass blades twined in for ornament, the way his grandma had done at the cabin on weekends, so the bouquet had structure. He came back and held them out across the deck rail.
The number ticked from 46 to 49. Then to 50. Then it shimmered, and *Tier: Friend* gave its little dissolve, and *Tier: Colleague* slid into place under her name. The locked AVAILABLE REQUESTS readout unlocked, lighting up amber. Two options were listed in muted text:
Request: Take Me Fishing on the Lake Request: Show Me Your Old World
Mira looked at him over the top of the third bouquet, her mouth tipped into a small pleased smile, the cross-armed annoyance gone instantly.
"That was a very good third bouquet," she said, brushing the grass-blade with her thumb.
"My grandma taught me."
"Tell your grandma I said thank you."
He huffed a small laugh. "My grandma's dead, Mira."
"Tell her anyway. Probably she can hear from where she is. People in the village swear by it." She buried her nose in the bouquet, breathed in, and beamed at him over the tops of the flowers. "Okay. What else are we doing today?"
Drew watched her face for a second.
The face was sunny, the same face she had been making at him this morning over the coffee-thing. Her portrait at the corner of his vision had its hands clasped at its chin and was looking at him with an expectant, fond patience, and her Mood readout was back to *Pleased*, soft green, and Drew was the only person on the deck who was currently thinking, in any active way, about the fact that he had grabbed her two minutes ago.
That was, he was already noticing, the test result.
"Mira," he said, watching her over the rail.
"Mhm?" She had her face in the bouquet again.
"Two minutes ago I grabbed your butt."
"I remember." She didn't look up.
"On purpose."
"I remember." She picked a yellow one out of the bunch, held it up, considered it, and tucked it back in.
"Just to see what would happen."
"That tracks." She lowered the bouquet, tilted her head, and looked at him over the top of it, genuinely curious. "Was the data good?"
He stared at her for a beat. "That's — Mira, that's all you've got to say about it?"
She tilted her head the other way. The portrait tilted with her.
"What else would I have to say about it?"
"I don't know. *Something.*"
"You bonked into a limit. The system made me yelp. You brought flowers." She gave a small easy shrug, genuinely already moved on. "Three good ones. Numbers came back up. Curriculum, Drew. We talked about this."
"Yeah, but —"
"But what?" She wasn't being defensive. It was a real question.
He didn't have a good answer to that, because he was not entirely sure what the *but* was. The *but* was somewhere in his chest, and he did not enjoy noticing it there, because the *but* was a thing from his old life, where you did not grab a woman who had not invited you to grab her, and where, if you did, she was upset for considerably longer than four seconds and considerably more than seven points worth, and where she did not then bury her nose in the apology bouquet and ask you what *else* the two of you were doing today.
The *but* was not a thing Mira had any obligation to. It was a thing he had brought with him, in his luggage, from the country he no longer lived in.
He tried again anyway.
"You're not — you're really not bothered."
"I was bothered. I yelped. You fixed it." She frowned briefly, the way someone frowns when asked the same question they've already answered three times. "Drew, am I supposed to be more bothered?"
"I — I don't know."
"Because if I'm supposed to be more bothered, I'll try." She said it the way you'd offer to take off your shoes if a host asked. "But the system says we're square, and I don't really feel like I'm not square, so I think we're square. Are we square?"
He looked at her for a beat. "We're square."
"Okay." She brightened, the small frown gone. "Then I'm going to put these in water. Hold these."
She handed him the third bouquet, scooped up the basket of laundry off the bench, and went into the shop. She left the back door open behind her. He could hear her, over the next thirty seconds, locating a vase, filling the vase, stepping back to look at the vase, adjusting the vase. The portrait at the corner of his vision sat with him on the deck, watching the door, perfectly content.
Drew sat on the bench with the bouquet across his lap.
He thought about the fact that he was, very quietly, a little uncomfortable.
He thought about the fact that the discomfort was, he was almost sure, the only discomfort currently on this deck. Mira was not feeling it. The portrait was not feeling it. The system, by every readout he could see, was not flagging anything. The discomfort was located in him, specifically, and was the small mismatch between the rules of the country he had grown up in and the rules of the country he had apparently just moved to.
He thought about Tracy on her couch, three or four Thanksgivings ago, with her glass of wine and her Switch in her hands, pressing a button to give a pixel-rendered woman a flower she'd already given her three times this week.
*This is my afterlife*, he thought. *I died in a kayak. I am thirty-one years old and I died in a kayak, and the only people who would be upset about what just happened on this deck are people who are not on this deck.*
He thought about that for a beat longer.
He gave himself a small, slightly queasy shrug of permission, and decided that this was, on balance, fine. The girl was unbothered. The system was unbothered. The discomfort was his, and his discomfort was his to manage. He was, apparently, allowed.
The shrug did not entirely settle him. He was going to have to sit with the not-settling for a while. But the part of him that had been weighing whether to keep playing had, somewhere in the last forty seconds, quietly stopped weighing.
Mira came back out onto the deck.
"Okay," she said, dusting her palms on her overalls. "What's next."
"One more thing," Drew said.
"Mhm?"
He had one more thing he wanted to ask. He had been turning it in his head while she was inside with the vase, and he had decided, while he was turning it, that he was going to keep asking the questions. The asking was the point.
"Mira, this is going to come out wrong. Are you — uh. Anatomically. The same as a human?"
She blinked at him.
"I don't know what that means."
"I'm asking whether you're, uh. Whether you have. Genitals. Nipples. The same equipment."
Her face went pink.
It went pink quickly, in two soft patches across her cheekbones, and her ears went pink at the tips, and the portrait at the corner of his vision went a corresponding color across the cheeks of her cartoon face. Drew watched it happen closely, intent on something he hadn't yet seen and was going to be thinking about for a while. The blush was, in his quiet professional opinion as a man, an enormously effective blush. He suspected the system had been a long time in building it.
"*Hey,*" she said. Quietly. Not the *hey* of yesterday. A different *hey*.
The number on the panel dropped from 50 to 44. The Mood flickered amber, *Embarrassed*, and settled.
Drew did not stand up. He had a bouquet on his lap.
"Mira, I'll ask it again, more politely, in a minute. I'm just going to give you back the bouquet."
He held it back out to her.
The number ticked from 44 to 47. The amber on the Mood readout faded back toward green. She took the flowers, breathed into them, and looked at him over the tops with an expression that was about three-quarters fond and one-quarter exasperated.
"You're going to ask it again," she said, watching him over the bouquet.
"I am going to ask it again."
"Because you want to know." She wasn't asking, she was confirming.
"Because I want to know."
"Okay." She set the bouquet down on the rail, leaned her elbows next to it, and arranged her face in the patient, slightly amused expression of a tutor walking a student back through a problem. "Ask."
He took a breath. "Are you, anatomically — "
"Yes."
"Yes?"
"Yes." She kept her elbows on the rail. "I have everything. Mostly-elf woman of twenty-two who has been twenty-two for a long time. I have not had children, because I can't, not with anyone in this village. The system only allows children with a protagonist. That's the rule. Every villager you've ever met was born to a protagonist. Not generations back, but direct, her actual father. He passed through, fathered her, moved on, the way mine did. We're not a downstream crop, Drew, we're the daughters. We grow up where we grow up, and we wait."
He sat with that for a second, hands resting on his knees. "That is — that is a wild sentence to drop into casual conversation, Mira."
"You asked," she said, the small smile pulling at her mouth.
"I did. Yeah, I did." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Mira, can I ask you a follow-up?"
"Don't ask me how it works."
"How does it w — "
He had reached, while he was talking, and poked her once in the breast with one finger. Lightly. The way a man pokes a balloon to confirm it is, in fact, a balloon.
"*Hey.*"
The number ticked from 47 to 44.
Drew pulled the hand back and lifted both of them in a not-sorry surrender. "Reflex."
"That one's not for today," she said, mouth fighting a small twitch.
"Got it."
He patted her on the head, which was for-today, apparently. The number ticked from 44 to 45.
The portrait at the corner of his vision rolled her eyes at him fondly, and the actual Mira beside him did the same. Drew had spent the better part of his morning earning and spending affection in equal measure, and he found that he was grinning.
"Go give Branwen her logs," Mira said, straightening up off the rail and giving him a small shove toward the deck stairs. "You owe her logs. Come home for dinner. Please."
"I will," he said.
"Mhm." She didn't sound convinced, but she was smiling.
He kissed her on the top of her head on his way out, briefly, because she was leaning on the rail with the bouquet beside her elbow and it was the move. The number ticked from 45 to 48. The portrait at the corner of his vision blushed all the way down to her collarbone, and so did the actual Mira. Drew had a pile of logs in his inventory and a long afternoon ahead of him. He picked up his axe and went.
Banjo went with him. The portrait stayed.
